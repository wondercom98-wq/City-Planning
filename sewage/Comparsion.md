Here's where things stand across all three cities — NYC's dealing with an aging combined sewer system under increasing storm loads, Boston is at a major inflection point with a new federal plan, and London just finished the project NYC is still years away from.

## New York City

**Scale of the problem:** NYC's sewer infrastructure was designed to handle a "five-year storm" of 1.75 inches of rain per hour, but the city was hit by five such events in 2023 alone, and projections suggest the new normal for a five-year storm will soon be 2.1 inches per hour. Over 70% of the city remains impervious surface, so heavy rain has nowhere to go but into basements, subways, and waterways via combined sewer overflows (CSOs). A striking real-world example: in May 2026, flooding in Hollis, Queens reached depths of up to 46 inches from roughly 2 inches of aggregate rainfall, with rainfall rates hitting 1.7 inches per minute in some areas — faster than urban drainage systems were ever designed for.

**What's being funded:**
- The city has an $8.2 billion capital plan for sewer upgrades, complemented by a newer $108 million investment (announced March 2026) to replace more than 6,700 catch basins over the next decade, building on a $20 million purchase of 40 modernized catch basin cleaning trucks.
- A $108 million citywide sewer upgrade plan includes a 4-million-gallon underground storage tank in Gowanus designed to capture sewage that would otherwise spill into the Gowanus Canal during heavy storms.
- On the coastal side, a $550 million Brooklyn Bridge-Montgomery Coastal Resilience project — floodwalls, deployable flip-up barriers, elevated roadways — is under construction in Lower Manhattan, targeting fall 2026 completion and designed to protect against 100-year storm surges through the 2050s.
- A $1.7 billion Fire Island to Montauk Point project will cover 83 miles of Long Island's south shore to stem erosion and improve storm resiliency, though the federal government's suspension of FEMA's Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities program halted roughly $200 million in funding that would have supported related projects.
- State-level: Governor Hochul's 2026 State of the State proposed a five-year, $3.75 billion water infrastructure commitment, which would bring total state water investment to nearly $10 billion since 2017.

**Gap:** Green infrastructure (rain gardens, permeable pavement, green roofs) is present but described by advocates as still secondary to the "gray" capital plan — annual maintenance for a green roof alone runs roughly 2-3% of its initial construction cost, which is part of why distributed, small-scale green storage hasn't scaled as fast as the sewer capital plan.

## Boston

Boston's approach is coastal-flood-focused rather than sewer-capacity-focused, and it just hit a milestone:

- A draft Coastal Storm Risk Management Feasibility Study, released with the Army Corps of Engineers, outlines over 20 infrastructure projects along 16 miles of Boston's 47-mile coastline, aiming to permanently close 20 long-term flood pathways.
- USACE estimates it will cost about $10 billion for baseline infrastructure to close all of Boston's coastal flood pathways, while the study projects this would prevent roughly $41 billion in future flood damage. The Corps estimates every dollar spent on flood prevention returns about $4 in avoided losses.
- About 40% of Boston residents currently live in an area at risk of flooding before the end of the century, and Boston could secure up to 65% of construction costs from the federal government — leaving the city to figure out financing for the remaining 35%, a question the Boston Green Ribbon Commission is spending a year studying.
- Notably, the plan leans heavily on hard infrastructure — floodwalls and building retrofits — with relatively little emphasis on wetlands or living shorelines, and stormwater flooding (as opposed to coastal surge) is only lightly addressed. Public comment on the draft runs through September 18, 2026.

**Compared to NYC:** Boston is earlier-stage (draft plan, final version to Congress by 2028) but structurally larger in ambition, and far more dependent on a single federal partner (USACE) for both design and cost-share.

## London

London is the clear outlier — it just finished the project:

- The Thames Tideway Tunnel, a £4.6 billion, 25km "super sewer," became fully connected in 2025, working alongside the Lee Tunnel to give the combined system 1.6 million m³ of capacity.
- With full connection, roughly 95% of storm sewage spills into the Thames are now being stopped, and as of the tunnel's activation, over 5.5 million m³ of sewage had already been captured — a live tracker continues to publish updated capture totals.
- Cost overrun is notable: the project was initially budgeted at £4.2bn but ended up costing about £5bn, paid for by Thames Water customers through bill increases of roughly £25/year over several decades.
- Caveat: critics note that with worsening climate change, the tunnel's capacity may have a limited effective lifespan — it was sized for historical rainfall patterns, not future intensification.

## Quick comparison

| | New York City | Boston | London |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core problem | Combined sewer overflow + inland flash flooding | Coastal surge + sea-level rise | Combined sewer overflow (mostly solved) |
| Flagship investment | ~$8.2B sewer capital plan + billions in coastal projects | $10B USACE coastal plan (draft) | £4.6-5B Tideway tunnel (complete) |
| Status | Mid-implementation, patchwork of projects | Draft/planning stage, final report due 2028 | Complete and operational since 2025 |
| Funding model | City + state (NY State $3.75B/5yr) + some federal | 65% federal cost-share proposed, 35% still unresolved | 100% ratepayer-funded via Thames Water bills |
| Measured result | No system-wide capture data yet | N/A (not yet built) | ~95% of CSO spills stopped |

The honest takeaway: London shows what "done" looks like — a single, massive, ratepayer-funded tunnel that solved the CSO problem outright. NYC and Boston are both still assembling theirs in pieces, and both face funding uncertainty at the federal level that London's fully local financing model never had to navigate. NYC's problem is also arguably harder than London's was, since NYC is fighting both flash-flood intensity *and* CSOs simultaneously, while Tideway was purely a sewage-capture project.
