Good question — let's break down the actual engineering building blocks that underlie every one of these systems, whether it's NYC's patchwork, Boston's coastal plan, or London's Tideway tunnel. There are really two separate systems that get bundled together under "sewage and flood recovery," and it helps to treat them separately before seeing how they interlock.The diagram breaks the problem into the two systems that keep getting bundled together, but which are actually engineered separately:

**1. Sewage/stormwater system (the vertical stack)**

- **Collection network** — catch basins and storm drains at street level, feeding into underground pipes. This is the part most people never think about, but it's where capacity gets exceeded first: if a catch basin is clogged or the pipe beneath it is undersized for today's rainfall intensity, water backs up onto the street before it ever reaches a treatment plant.
- **Storage & treatment** — this is the part cities are racing to build now. A CSO storage tunnel (or tank, like Gowanus's 4-million-gallon tank) acts as a shock absorber: instead of untreated sewage overflowing straight into a river during a storm, it gets held underground and released to the treatment plant once the storm passes and capacity frees up. London's Tideway tunnel is this exact component, just built at a massive scale — 25km long, holding the entire city's excess flow.
- **Discharge** — treated water released to the river or harbor, ideally with overflow now a rare event instead of a weekly one.

**2. Coastal/storm-surge defense (the separate box)**

This is architecturally unrelated to the sewer system — it's not about water your city generates (rain, waste), it's about water trying to come in from outside (tides, storm surge, sea-level rise). Floodwalls, deployable gates, and elevated infrastructure belong here. This is what Boston's $10B USACE plan and NYC's Two Bridges/Battery projects are actually building — sewage upgrades solve a completely different problem than a floodwall does, which is why cities need both budgets running in parallel rather than one solving the other.

The reason NYC's challenge is harder than London's was: London only had to solve box 1. NYC has to solve both simultaneously, on a coastline, while retrofitting a much larger and older combined system underneath one of the densest cities in the world.
